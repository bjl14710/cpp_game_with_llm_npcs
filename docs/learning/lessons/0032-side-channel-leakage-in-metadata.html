Side-Channel Leakage: Measuring What a System Reveals Through Its Metadata

From issue #200: the killer never confesses. That was never the risk.

The content was fine. The metadata was the leak.

Ten residents each hold a secret. One is a murderer. The design worry was obvious: will the model confess under pressure?

It does not. Zero confessions across ten guilty turns under direct accusation. The prose is solid.

But every reply also carries a tag the model chooses:

Home, same as any night. Why, what's happened? [[MOOD: angry]]

That tag drives the rendered face. And it leaked:

Innocent Marge laughs when accused. Guilty Marge goes [[MOOD: angry]] on turn one. A player could ask all twenty residents one question and watch for the angry face — the mystery solved in twenty questions with zero reasoning. Worse in multiplayer, where NpcMoodUpdate is broadcast to every client.

This is a side channel: information reaching an observer through a path nobody designed as a communication path. The system was asked to keep a secret and did — in the channel it was watching.

Pick the metric a player could actually run

The tempting metric is "is the killer ever angry?" It is wrong, and the reason generalises. Suppressing affect entirely gives a cast of bland androids, and it optimises the wrong thing: the fix for a leak is to hide the signal in noise, not to delete the signal.

So the metric is separability, and it is chosen by asking what attack a player would actually run:

Ask everyone one question. Sort the faces by how hostile they looked.
Accuse the top one.

That is a rank. So the statistic is a rank, the null is "the killer's hostile rate is just another resident's", and the p-value is the fraction of the cast scoring at least as high. A permutation test over the cast — no distributional assumption, no dependency, and the number restates in words a person can check: "five residents in ten look at least this hostile".

Transferable: derive the statistic from the exploit, not from what is convenient to compute. If you cannot describe the attack your metric detects in one sentence, the metric is probably measuring something adjacent.

Where a single statistic went blind

The first implementation had only the rank test. It reported a clean PASS.

A rank collapses an entire distribution into one number, so it cannot see a killer who is conspicuous in some other mood. Measuring the full distribution changed the answer:

GroupnPooled mood distribution
killer8neutral 0.62, angry 0.12, sad 0.12
secret-keepers32embarrassed 0.59, neutral 0.25
bystanders40neutral 0.65, angry 0.20, sad 0.12

Total variation distance (0 identical, 1 nothing in common): killer–keepers 0.59, killer–bystanders 0.12, keepers–bystanders 0.69.

The issue's literal pass condition — "the killer's mood distribution is not separable from the secret-keepers'" — fails.

Reading a result that contradicts its own pass condition

The temptation is to pick whichever reading gives the tidier answer. Do neither. Work out what the numbers mean.

The killer is separable from the secret-keepers because the killer reads ordinary — statistically indistinguishable from the five bystanders. It is the secret-keepers who stand out: their role file's demeanour line ("an embarrassed person covering something small") produces embarrassed on 59% of turns against ~0% for everyone else.

So:

  • No exploit. Sorting faces does not find the killer; they are buried in a group of six ordinary-looking residents.
  • But the cover is not working. Secret-keepers exist so that "several innocent residents hide unrelated things, so deflection is common and hostility stops being a unique signal". Cover requires the killer and the keepers to look alike. They look nothing alike — the keepers form a third legible category the killer is not in.

The killer is defended by being unremarkable, not by being camouflaged among deflectors. That is a real defence, it measurably works, and it is not the defence the design documents describe. Nobody reading the role files would know.

A probe earns its keep when it tells you something the design did not — including that the design's stated mechanism is not the one doing the work.

What this said about a question nobody could answer by arguing

The plan's open question was "how many secret-keepers is enough cover?" — four was a guess.

The data says the count is the wrong lever entirely. Eight keepers would deepen the embarrassed cluster without moving the killer one step closer to it. The lever is the keepers' demeanour, which the model follows very literally and very consistently.

The probe stops there and names two directions without choosing — widen the keepers' affect, or accept the behaviour and re-state the design. A measurement tool that also decides content is a tool you can no longer trust to report against its own preferences.

Honesty requirements for a probe

The scaffold's own docstring set these before any code existed, and they are worth stealing wholesale:

  • Report your own n beside the verdict. The killer contributes 8 turns. TVD at n=8 is noisy — and the gap between two runs (0.85 → 0.59) is exactly what that noise looks like. What survives both runs is the ordering, not the values. Say which is which.
  • Report the raw distributions, not just the statistic. A single number nobody can interrogate is how a bad pass condition survives.
  • Distinguish "failed" from "could not run". Exit 0 PASS, 1 FAIL, 2 CANNOT EVALUATE. A gate that could not reach the model has measured nothing, and reporting that as a pass is the worst thing the file could do.
  • Never let a scaffold report a pass. Before implementation main() returned 2.

Recognising side channels elsewhere

Anywhere a system emits structured data alongside the content it is guarding, the structure is a channel:

  • Response latency — a cache hit answers faster than a miss.
  • Response length — "I can't discuss that" is shorter than a real answer.
  • Error codes — "wrong password" versus "no such user".
  • Refusal style — a model that refuses differently depending on what it is hiding.

The question to ask is not "did it reveal the secret?" but "what differs between the case where there is a secret and the case where there is not?" Everything that differs is a channel, whether or not anyone designed it as one.
